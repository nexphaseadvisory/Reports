01  The Bottom Line
The week's filings and approvals together make one point clearly: queue position and zoning approval are no longer the hard constraints, because the obstacles now live in regulatory gaps, ballot timelines, and transmission underinvestment that standard project schedules do not price. Duke's large-load tariff filing in the Carolinas landing without the clean transition tariff that Amazon, Google, and Microsoft have been pursuing since 2024 is the clearest example, but Ohio's ballot-issue risk against projects that already cleared zoning and the analyst case for grid-enhancing technologies that transmission owners are not yet committing capital to follow the same logic. Developers whose 2025 and 2026 schedules depend on new transmission, clean-attributed tariff structures, or community approvals that can be reopened through political mechanisms should be stress-testing those assumptions now, before financing conversations force the issue.

05PolicyDuke Energy's large load tariff filing in the Carolinas omits a clean transition tariff despite hyperscaler and stakeholder support.The Read▾
Duke Energy · 2026-10-09
Duke's large-load tariff filing in the Carolinas arrived without the clean transition tariff that Amazon, Google, and Microsoft had been working toward since a 2024 White House commitment. That is a two-year gap between a public announcement and a formal filing, and the CTT still is not in the proposal. The North Carolina attorney general and CEBA have both pushed for it on the record. The question now is whether the commission directs Duke to file one separately, or whether the hyperscalers that need clean-attributed power in the Carolinas are left negotiating around a tariff structure that was not designed for their procurement requirements. This is the kind of regulatory gap that stalls large-load interconnection agreements even after the queue position is secured.
